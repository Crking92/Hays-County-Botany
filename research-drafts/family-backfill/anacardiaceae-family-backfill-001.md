# Anacardiaceae family backfill — Central Texas visual learner

**Status:** ready for first family module build; visual polish pending.

## Where to look first

Start with a **woody shrub, small tree, or woody vine** with **alternate leaves and no stipules**. Then look for the family combination:

**resinous tissues + small usually 5-part flowers + superior ovary + drupe fruit.**

Do **not** crush unknown plants to check the resin or odor. Poison ivy is in this family.

## The most important local split: Rhus vs Toxicodendron

### Rhus — our sumacs
Think **red fruit + terminal clusters + species-specific leaf architecture**.

- *Rhus copallinum var. lanceolata*: many narrow leaflets + a conspicuously **winged rachis**
- *Rhus virens*: only 5–9 thick glossy entire leaflets + evergreen/semi-evergreen habit
- *Rhus aromatica*: exactly **3** often lobed/coarsely toothed leaflets + low suckering shrub
- *Rhus microphylla*: small odd-pinnate leaves on a dense twiggy shrub + winged rachis

### Toxicodendron — poison ivy complex
Think **3 leaflets + axillary clusters + pale fruit**, then look at growth habit.

*Poison ivy can climb with dense aerial rootlets, trail, or form a shrub.* Its fruits mature pale cream/white rather than the red hairy fruits of the local sumacs.

## Critical teaching correction

“Leaves of three, let it be” is a useful safety slogan, but it is **not an identification key**.

*Rhus aromatica* also has exactly three leaflets.

For a real field ID, add:

1. growth habit
2. aerial rootlets
3. fruit color
4. terminal vs axillary fruit/flower cluster
5. leaflet shape and petiolules

### Rhus aromatica
- low free-standing suckering shrub
- 3 aromatic, often lobed/toothed leaflets
- compact terminal flower clusters
- **red hairy drupes**

### Toxicodendron radicans
- vine, trailer, scrambling shrub, or low shrub
- 3 highly variable leaflets
- climbing stems may have dense aerial rootlets
- axillary flower/fruit clusters
- **pale whitish drupes**

## Family architecture worth teaching

- alternate leaves in our local genera
- no stipules
- simple/trifoliolate/pinnate leaf architecture exists across the family
- resin canals in multiple tissues
- small flowers, commonly 5-merous
- nectar disk
- superior ovary
- usually a 1-seeded drupe locally

## Best local anchor sequence

### 1. Prairie flameleaf sumac — *Rhus copallinum var. lanceolata*
Use this to teach the word **rachis**. The wings running between the leaflet pairs are one of the best plant-part characters in the project.

### 2. Evergreen sumac — *Rhus virens*
Use this to teach leaflet number and texture: 5–9 thick, leathery, glossy, mostly entire leaflets.

### 3. Fragrant sumac — *Rhus aromatica*
This is the poison-ivy trap card. Exactly three leaflets does not automatically equal poison ivy.

Current POWO/FNA treatment places the old *Rhus trilobata* concept under *Rhus aromatica var. aromatica*, so the source-pool *R. trilobata* rows are now flagged as taxonomy duplicates rather than treated as separate current species.

### 4. Littleleaf sumac — *Rhus microphylla*
Useful regional structural comparison: small odd-pinnate leaves, winged rachis, and tight reddish-orange fruit clusters. Its Hays Purola occurrence is from an introduced-site record, so it stays a comparison until strict range review.

### 5. Eastern poison ivy — *Toxicodendron radicans*
Use this for:
- trifoliolate leaf
- asymmetric lateral leaflet bases
- aerial rootlets
- axillary inflorescence
- pale drupe
- plant-safety training

## Confusable families

**Sapindaceae:** can also have compound leaves and small flowers. Anacardiaceae commonly has resinous tissues; Sapindaceae usually does not. Local fruits also help enormously.

**Rutaceae:** often strongly aromatic, but look for the tiny **pellucid oil-gland dots** in the leaves and the citrus/rank-citrus odor. Rutaceae lacks the resinous sap character.

**Rhamnaceae:** often has simple leaves and drupes, but **stipules are usually present**. Local Rhus/Toxicodendron lacks stipules and is usually compound-leaved.
