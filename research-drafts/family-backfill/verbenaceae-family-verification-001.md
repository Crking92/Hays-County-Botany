## Stop using square stems

Square stems do not separate these families.

Both Verbenaceae and Lamiaceae can have:
- opposite leaves
- aromatic foliage
- quadrangular stems
- tubular bilateral flowers
- four stamens

For our learner, move instead to:

**inflorescence architecture → ovary/style → fruit**

## Verbenaceae pattern

- opposite/decussate leaves
- no stipules
- usually **indeterminate racemose** inflorescences
- spikes or compact heads are common
- superior ovary
- **terminal style**
- fruit may be dry or fleshy
- fruit can split into 2 or 4 mericarps, or become a drupe with pyrenes

## Lamiaceae comparison

The classic local mint-family pattern is:
- cymes / verticillasters / thyrses
- deeply 4-lobed ovary
- usually gynobasic style
- usually four nutlets

Those are patterns, not universal absolutes, but together they are much better than stem shape.

## Local fruit lesson

### Verbena / Glandularia
Dry fruit that matures into **four one-seeded mericarps** inside the persistent calyx.

### Phyla nodiflora
Tiny fruit resolving into **two pyrenes/mericarps**.

### Lantana
A genuinely **fleshy drupe** with a hard 2-celled stone/endocarp.

That three-way fruit card should make Verbenaceae much easier to remember.

## Local genera

### Verbena
Think elongated spikes.

*Verbena halei* is a current accepted Texas-native anchor.

### Glandularia
Think contracted flower head rather than a long Verbena spike.

Current POWO accepts both *Glandularia bipinnatifida* and *G. pumila*.

### Phyla
Think creeping mat, rooting nodes and tiny compact heads.

*Phyla nodiflora* gives the useful two-part fruit exception.

### Lantana
Think woody shrub, rough opposite leaves, dense flower heads and fleshy purple-black drupes.

Our native anchor is **Lantana urticoides**.

### Aloysia
Think strongly aromatic woody shrub with narrow axillary racemes/spikes.

**Aloysia gratissima** is native into Texas.
