# Berberidaceae family backfill — Central Texas visual learner

**Status:** ready for first family module build; visual polish pending.

## Where to look first

For our native Central Texas lesson, start with **Berberis**:

- evergreen prickly shrub
- leathery compound leaves with spine-tipped margins
- bright yellow inner wood/tissue
- small yellow flowers
- berry fruit

Then zoom into the flower.

The memorable microscopic character is the **anther**.

In *Berberis*, the pollen sacs open with **two little valves hinged at the top**.

That is much more useful than the old shorthand “6 tepals + 6 stamens.”

## Better flower anatomy

For local Berberis, teach:

- several petaloid sepals in multiple whorls
- **6 true petals**
- nectar glands near the petal bases
- **6 stamens**, opposite the petals
- superior ovary
- anthers opening by hinged valves

The stamens are also touch-sensitive and can move toward the pistil after stimulation. That is a great enrichment fact, but the anther structure is the better ID character.

## Agarita — Berberis trifoliolata

The clean species form gives you:

- exactly **3 leaflets**
- rigid blue-green foliage
- strongly spine-tipped leaflet margins
- fragrant yellow flowers
- bright red berries
- yellow inner wood

Current POWO accepts *Berberis trifoliolata*. The old **Mahonia trifoliolata** name is a synonym, so those source rows should not become separate learner cards.

## Texas barberry — Berberis swaseyi

Think:

- usually **5–9 leaflets**
- evergreen Edwards Plateau shrub
- lax racemes
- white or red, somewhat glaucous berries
- berries often **hollow**
- very local/conservation-sensitive

FNA treats it as endemic to the Edwards Plateau, and TPWD lists it as a Species of Greatest Conservation Need, G3/S3.

## The hybrid lesson

This may be one of the best real-botany lessons in the whole project.

Agarita and Texas barberry **hybridize in Central Texas**.

At the Purola Preserve in Hays County, intermediates with 5–7 leaflets have been documented.

So:

**3 leaflets = good first clue for agarita**  
**7–9 leaflets = good first clue for Texas barberry**  
**5–7 leaflets = slow down**

Then examine:
- bracteoles
- fruit
- stamen morphology

This teaches exactly why one-character identification can fail.

## Nandina — the family exception

*Nandina domestica* belongs to Berberidaceae too, but looks radically different:

- cane-like shrub
- huge 2–3-pinnate leaves
- entire leaflets
- white flower panicles
- red berries

Most importantly:

**its anthers open by longitudinal slits, NOT hinged valves.**

So the learner should say:

> Valvate anthers are a strong Berberis character, not an absolute Berberidaceae character.

Nandina is native to China, introduced in Texas, and listed by TexasInvasives. It stays only as a labeled invasive comparison.

## Oregon grape — Berberis aquifolium

The pool has one encounter row.

Current POWO gives its native range as southwestern Canada through northern California, so it is **not a Texas native anchor**.

Keep it only as an out-of-range/horticultural comparison if it proves useful.

## Source-pool cleanup

The family had been undercounted because Mahonia and Nandina rows were not mapped into Berberidaceae.

The live pool now contains **6 source rows across 3 source genera**, but current taxonomy resolves these to four accepted concepts:

- *Berberis trifoliolata*
- *Berberis swaseyi*
- *Berberis aquifolium*
- *Nandina domestica*

The two Mahonia rows are preserved as synonym/source history rather than deleted.
