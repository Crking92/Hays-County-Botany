## First rule

The thing that looks like one flower is usually a **capitulum** — an entire inflorescence.

Look for:

- **involucre** — the basket around the head
- **phyllaries** — each individual bract making that basket
- **receptacle** — the shared platform
- individual **florets**
- fused anther tube
- inferior ovary
- **cypsela**
- **pappus**

## Ray florets are not petals

Each 'petal' around a sunflower-looking head is an individual **ray floret**.

The center is made of separate **disk florets**.

And those florets do not all have to be sexually equivalent.

## Four head types to learn

### Radiate
Ray florets outside + disk florets inside.

Local examples:
- Helianthus
- Thelesperma
- Rudbeckia
- Ratibida
- Echinacea
- many Coreopsis
- usually Solidago

### Discoid
No rays. All visible florets are tubular/actinomorphic.

Local examples:
- Liatris
- Cirsium
- Conoclinium
- Eupatorium
- Vernonia
- Baccharis

### Disciform
No showy ray corollas, but there are still two floret types.

Outer florets are slender and usually female; central florets are tubular.

**Pluchea** is our local example.

### Liguliflorous
Every floret is strap-shaped, bisexual and usually fertile.

Classic dandelion/chicory architecture.

Our current 28-genus pool does not contain a classic Cichorieae anchor, so this stays a family-breadth comparison card.

## Pappus = calyx

The pappus is not just 'fluff.'

It is the **modified calyx**.

It can be:
- hairs
- bristles
- awns
- scales
- a short crown
- or completely absent

So do not teach 'Asteraceae seed = parachute.'

## Cypsela, not just achene

The one-seeded dry fruit is best called a **cypsela** because it develops from an **inferior ovary**.

A lot of floras still use 'achene,' so the learner should recognize both terms.

## The fused-anther trick

Asteraceae usually has five anthers fused into a cylinder around the style.

As the style elongates, it moves through that tube and pushes/sweeps pollen upward.

That is one of the family's strongest microscopic characters.

## Major exceptions

### Ambrosia — ragweed
Forget the daisy look.

- male and female heads are separate
- male heads occur in spikes/racemes
- female heads are tiny and usually axillary
- female corolla can be absent
- pappus absent
- female involucre hardens around the fruit

### Baccharis
Another major lesson:

- shrub
- discoid heads
- usually **dioecious**
- separate male and female plants
- only female heads make cypselae
- fruiting females often become very fluffy from the pappus

### Silphium
This may be one of our best reproductive lessons.

In FNA treatment:
- ray florets = **female and fertile**
- disk florets = **functionally male**

So only the rays make cypselae.

The center of the 'sunflower' is not where the mature fruits come from.

### Coreopsis
Do not even assume one rule for ray-floret sexuality across the genus.

Rays can be:
- neutral
- styliferous but sterile
- female and fertile

### Cirsium
Thistles are clean discoid Asteraceae:

- no rays
- lots of phyllaries
- often spine-tipped phyllaries
- plumose pappus

### Solidago
Usually radiate, but some heads can be discoid.

Another warning that head type should be observed, not assumed.
