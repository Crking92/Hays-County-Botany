# Ebenaceae family backfill — Central Texas visual learner

**Status:** ready for first family module build; visual polish pending.

## Where to look first

For our local learner, think:

**alternate simple entire leaf + urn/bell-shaped flower + big persistent calyx under a fleshy berry**

That persistent calyx is the best family teaching character.

The flower's calyx does not disappear after bloom. It stays attached, often enlarges, and ends up looking like a leafy saucer beneath the persimmon.

## Texas persimmon — Diospyros texana

This is already our canonical local Ebenaceae anchor.

### Look here first
**The bark.**

Texas persimmon has:
- smooth pale gray bark
- exfoliating/mottled patches
- white, gray and pink tones underneath

Then confirm:
- tiny thick alternate leaves
- rounded or slightly notched leaf tips
- hairy/tomentose lower leaf surface
- small black mature berry
- persistent 5-lobed calyx

FNA separates it from common persimmon with several really nice microscopic characters:
- 5-part flower
- staminate anthers opening by short subapical slits
- pistillate flowers without staminodes
- hairy ovary
- black pubescent fruit

## Common persimmon — Diospyros virginiana

This is our comparison species pending strict target-zone review.

Look for:
- much larger tree
- dark deeply furrowed **blocky/alligator bark**
- much larger leaves
- pointed leaf tips
- larger orange/yellow to reddish fruits
- persistent 4-lobed calyx

FNA gives:
- 4-part flowers
- staminate anthers opening along their full length
- pistillate flowers usually with about 8 staminodes
- mostly glabrous ovary/fruit
- much larger berries

Kew/FNA recognize it as Texas-native. Texas A&M describes **Travis County as the western edge of its natural habitat**, but that does not yet satisfy our project's three-exact-target-county rule.

## Killer comparison card

### D. texana
- pale smooth peeling bark
- tiny rounded leaves
- 5-part flowers
- black hairy 1.5–2.5 cm fruit

### D. virginiana
- dark blocky bark
- large pointed leaves
- 4-part flowers
- orange/yellow 3–5 cm fruit

That is an extremely learnable species pair.

## Male vs female flowers

Diospyros flowers are usually unisexual.

A useful general pattern:
- staminate flowers often occur in small clusters
- pistillate flowers are commonly solitary
- pistillate flowers later show exactly where the persistent calyx beneath the fruit came from

### Important D. texana source conflict

There is a real disagreement in the sources.

FNA, the U.S. Forest Service, and Texas A&M describe Texas persimmon as **dioecious**.

The detailed UT Williamson County treatment labels it **monoecious** and describes male and female flowers on the same plant.

So the learner should teach the flower anatomy, but **not use plant-level sex system alone as an ID character** until we reconcile that discrepancy.

## Fruit anatomy

Teach the progression:

1. pistillate flower
2. persistent calyx
3. young berry
4. mature berry
5. cut fruit
6. multiple large seeds

### Texas persimmon
Small black staining berry, hairy outside, several seeds.

### Common persimmon
Larger yellow/orange/red berry, mostly smooth, several large brown seeds.

## Main confusers

### Sapotaceae
Our local Sideroxylon can also have alternate entire leaves and fleshy fruit.

Look for:
- milky latex
- thorny short shoots
- a few large glossy seeds

Diospyros instead gives the huge persistent calyx.

### Aquifoliaceae
Ilex also has alternate leaves and fleshy fruit.

But:
- Ilex = small drupe with several hard pyrenes
- Diospyros = berry with several large seeds + enlarged persistent calyx

## Best first visual sequence

1. D. texana bark
2. D. virginiana bark
3. leaf comparison
4. male/staminate flowers
5. female/pistillate flower
6. label the calyx
7. young fruit with calyx
8. mature fruit comparison
9. fruit cross-sections/seeds
10. Ebenaceae vs Sapotaceae vs Aquifoliaceae
