# Cupressaceae family backfill — Central Texas visual learner

**Status:** ready for first family module build; visual polish pending.

## Where to look first

Start with **foliage + cone type**.

### Juniperus
- evergreen
- mature foliage mostly tiny **scale leaves**
- branchlets commonly 3–4 angled
- small pollen cones
- female **fleshy seed cones**

The first teaching correction should be:

> A juniper “berry” is not a berry or a fruit. It is a fleshy seed cone.

### Taxodium
- deciduous/seasonally cladoptosic
- linear leaves on feathery short shoots
- pendent pollen-cone panicles
- **woody round seed cones**

That makes Cupressaceae visually memorable because our two local genera barely look like they belong together.

## Family architecture

Cupressaceae is a conifer family. There are no flowers, ovaries, or fruits.

Instead look for:
- pollen cones
- seed cones
- cone scales fused with their subtending bracts
- exposed ovules/seeds
- scale-, awl-, or linear leaves
- resinous/aromatic woody tissues

Most are evergreen, but **Taxodium is the big local exception**.

## Anchor 1 — Ashe juniper, Juniperus ashei

This is already a canonical project learner and remains the strongest local Cupressaceae anchor.

The best microscopic/close-up character against eastern redcedar is the **abaxial resin gland**:

**Ashe juniper:** gland strongly **raised and hemispheric**.

Also teach:
- dark-green mature scale leaves
- thin peeling bark
- small pollen cones
- dark-blue glaucous fleshy seed cones
- seed cones usually about 6–9 mm

The existing UT Hays page has exceptionally useful male- and female-cone study images, and Commons has a reusable Austin photograph of Ashe-juniper foliage plus pollen cones.

## Anchor 2 — eastern redcedar, Juniperus virginiana

Best comparison against Ashe juniper:

**Eastern redcedar:** abaxial gland **elliptic or elongate**, not strongly hemispherically raised.

Its fleshy seed cones also tend to be smaller, generally around **3–6(-7) mm**, versus 6–9 mm in Ashe juniper.

Useful images are already available for:
- mature scale foliage
- twig + seed cones
- pollen cone

Species-level deep review still needs to be completed before promotion.

## Anchor 3 — baldcypress, Taxodium distichum

This is the perfect “family exception” card.

Look for:
- deciduous conifer
- linear leaves
- short leafy shoots that are shed seasonally
- large pendent clusters/panicles of pollen cones
- spherical woody seed cones
- shield-shaped cone scales

Do not teach cypress knees as mandatory. They are useful when present, but Taxodium can occur without obvious pneumatophores.

Current POWO accepts **Taxodium distichum** with three varieties:
- var. distichum
- var. imbricarium
- var. mexicanum

The Master Plant Pool stays at species level until we do the dedicated local/infraspecific review.

## Joey Montezuma-cypress extraction

The new Joey video extraction is extremely useful here.

The video calls the plant **Taxodium mucronatum**. Under the current POWO treatment that name belongs under **Taxodium distichum var. mexicanum**.

For this learner it remains a **study comparison**, not a Hays-local species promotion.

Its frames give us an unusually complete reproductive sequence:

1. pollen-cone clusters
2. pollen-cone close-up
3. mature seed cone
4. cone breaking into scales
5. cone scale + seed

That may be the best visual source we have so far for teaching what a gymnosperm cone actually is.

## Juniperus vs Taxodium

### Juniperus
Evergreen + scale/awl foliage + fleshy berry-like cone.

### Taxodium
Deciduous + linear foliage + woody globose cone.

## Ashe juniper vs eastern redcedar

Use the leaf gland before overall tree shape:

**J. ashei**
- raised hemispheric gland
- scale leaves often 1–2 mm
- seed cones roughly 6–9 mm

**J. virginiana**
- elliptic/elongate gland
- scale leaves roughly 1–3 mm
- seed cones roughly 3–6(-7) mm

Tree silhouette and bark help, but the gland/cone combination is far safer for the learner.

## Confusable families

### Pinaceae
Usually obvious needles and classic woody cones. Cone scales are free from the bract and commonly bear two seeds.

### Taxaceae
The key difference is the **aril**: Taxaceae commonly has a single seed partly or wholly surrounded by fleshy tissue. Cupressaceae does not have an aril.
